# 03 Units of Change, Pacing, Timing, and Pauses

Updated: 2026-09-17. Checks for comic production and review. These are production proposals to select according to the project. For related film and screenwriting material, see [Sources and scope](../manga-sources.md).

<a id="r01"></a>

## R01 Distinguish units of change, structural milestones, and brief pauses

Treat story milestones, changes of course within a scene, and short pauses before a reply as different scales. When the same term describes them, record which scale is intended.

<a id="r02"></a>

## R02 Design pacing and timing separately

Consider pacing as the density of information and action, and timing as what readers learn at which point. Decide which you want to change before adding panels.

<a id="r03"></a>

## R03 Separate event time, depiction time, and reading time

Elapsed story time, the space devoted to depicting it, and actual reading time do not coincide. Show skipped time through visual changes and check reading time through a trial read.

<a id="r04"></a>

## R04 Describe changes with verbs and find repetition

List each panel's function with verbs such as search, notice, and hide. Where functions repeat, decide whether they provide needed emphasis or duplicate information.

When reusing a place, object, or action, distinguish the elements held constant for comparison from what changes this time. Check whether each return updates information or relationships. Not doing something a character previously did can also mark change.

<a id="r05"></a>

## R05 Make stimulus, recognition, choice, and response visible

Check `stimulus → what was learned → how it was interpreted → what is wanted → next action`. Combine noticing, understanding, and responding as far as clarity allows. There is no requirement to put each stage in a separate panel.

A sad face or loud voice alone does not reveal purpose. The same forceful delivery might reject, plead, or support. Do not treat a smile as conclusive proof of affection or resolution, or tears as conclusive proof of sadness; combine them with surrounding choices.

Where a reaction feels abrupt, check for missing clues to understanding or judgment.

Actually inspect the preceding and following material and identify the stimulus, the changed action, and its connection to what happens next. “Make it more expressive” alone does not identify a revision. If purpose is clear, a version without increased facial exaggeration may work.

<a id="r06"></a>

## R06 Allow anticipation before a turn and absorption after it

Before a major change, provide material for anticipation; afterward, provide reactions that absorb the change. Both need not be long. Allocate space to the experience readers should savor in that scene.

<a id="reader-anticipation"></a>

### Create anticipation for what comes next, then answer it

When choosing the next panel, consider what readers now know and what they want to confirm next. Create a question or expectation through setup and answer it through later images or explanation. Here, a payoff includes revealing an identity, witnessing a result, or connecting meaning, as well as laughter.

| Intended content | Interest or expectation to create first | Information that answers it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The result of an impact or major action | Suggest “What will happen?” through the starting motion or approaching change | Show the actual action, contact, and result |
| A character's or object's identity | Reveal a presence or partial features to prompt “Who or what is it?” | Show an identifiable appearance or clue |
| A character's expression or reaction | Establish their situation in a wider view to prompt interest in their face | Show the expression or small action close up |
| Surroundings or spatial relationships | Show a reaction or detail close up to prompt “What are they seeing?” or “Where are they?” | Reveal the subject or its surroundings in a wider view |
| A reason or circumstance | Lead with an intriguing result, statement, or reaction to prompt “Why?” | Supply an explanation relevant to that question |

For lengthy explanations, check that readers have a reason to want the answer, beyond the character's desire to speak. Setup does not necessarily make unnecessary explanation easier to read; focus on what answers the question.

This is not a rule to alternate close and wide views or always use two panels. If interest already exists, supply the answer directly. Distinguish mere confusion about a situation from curiosity about what follows an understood situation. In scenes prioritizing surprise or lingering feeling, choose whether to provide setup and when to give the answer.

In scripts and PNGs, ask what the earlier image makes readers want to know, what the later image or dialogue answers, and whether the answer is visible too early. Keep the agent's assessment separate from reader responses when evaluating whether the intended curiosity or satisfaction actually occurred.

<a id="r07"></a>

## R07 The longer the wait, the more expectation or danger should change

When extending a wait, depict approaching danger, growing expectation, or deepening uncertainty. If it only repeats the same state, compare a shorter version.

<a id="r08"></a>

## R08 Establish the scene's emotional focus through reaction order

The order of reactions to one event changes whose emotions readers follow. Decide where to return to the protagonist so the emotional focus remains clear even in an ensemble.

<a id="r09"></a>

## R09 Combine scene length with density

Review the density of dialogue-heavy, action-focused, and situational scenes in sequence. Look beyond differences in length to uneven demands from words and images.

| Dimension | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Text | Words, sentences, speakers, line breaks, and reading order |
| New information | New facts, goals, causal links, and relationships |
| Inference | Actions, true feelings, time, and reasons readers must supply |
| Transitions | The effort of reestablishing characters, place, time, and viewpoint |
| Time to absorb emotion | The need to take in an already understood event alongside the character |

Do not use panel count or text length as substitutes for all these dimensions. Few panels may require heavy explanation or inference, and wordless panels can convey a great deal.

### Reviewing pages with flashbacks, time skips, or simultaneous events

Only at panels where time changes, annotate the written script with scene/time, chronological order versus presentation order, time of narration versus time depicted, and cues for transitions and returns. No extra table is needed without a transition. Do not force chronological order or prohibit nested flashbacks.

Combine dialogue, character appearance, location, and object states so the depicted time can be followed visually. Black gutters alone do not establish clarity. When reading thumbnails or PNGs without explanation, compare the author's intended temporal relationships with those perceived by the reader. If the time is deliberately concealed, check where the design later makes it understandable.

## R10 Enter late and leave room for the necessary emotional aftermath

Begin where changes relevant to the scene's purpose start. At the end, judge whether readers need a reaction that helps them understand the result as well as the event's resolution.

<a id="r11"></a>

## R11 Arrange reader knowledge in three ways

For each character, separate known facts, believed interpretations, and desired outcomes. Choose whether readers know first, characters know first, or both discover simultaneously. Include reasons a character fails to notice.

Do not conceal information for its own sake. Decide whether the purpose is concern, a question, or surprise.

### Separate an explainer's claims, observed results, and hypotheses

Distinguish what an explainer claims, what was actually observed, and what characters hypothesize. When a result differs from a prediction, preserve prior facts and connect the revised hypothesis to the changed next action. The same check applies to rumors, explanations of abilities, and product value.

Sort important conditions by whether they concern activation, effects, costs, duration, or termination. This helps find conditions missing from an explanation of the outcome; it is not an instruction to add form fields.

<a id="r12"></a>

## R12 Place key words and decisive images where the reaction begins

Design whether readers first receive the crucial word or the decisive image. If a balloon gives away the conclusion early, change placement or panel divisions.

<a id="r13"></a>

## R13 Use page turns as information boundaries

When hiding information behind a page turn, check the actual starting page position and spreads. Do not assume the same effect in a viewer that shows the next page on the same screen.

<a id="r14"></a>

## R14 Record the reader's state when evaluating pauses

In trial reads, record not only “slow” or “fast,” but what readers were waiting for, where they understood, and where they reread. Keep impressions separate from authorial intent and use both for revision.

<a id="r15"></a>

## R15 Check the type and function of silence

“No spoken dialogue” means no voiced lines. “No verbal description” means no dialogue, internal monologue, narration, or written words necessary for understanding. Check sounds, breathing, and symbols separately. These are not identical to “no lettering of any kind.”

In silence, identify what changes: action, recognition, hesitation, waiting, shared understanding, elapsed time, relationships, or lingering feeling. More repetitions of a reaction do not prove that readers have more time to feel it. Do not adopt a default preference or numerical ratio for long silent passages.
