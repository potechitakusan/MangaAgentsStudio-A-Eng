# 04 Scenes, Dialogue, and Props

Updated: 2026-09-17. Checks for comic production and review. These are production proposals to select according to the project. For related film and screenwriting material, see [Sources and scope](../manga-sources.md).

<a id="d01"></a>

## D01 Write the difference between the beginning and end of a scene

Describe the before and after in one sentence each, then check changes in purpose, knowledge, and relationships. If little changes, consider whether the unit serves as introduction, rest, or emotional aftermath. Allow units to do work other than comedy. Distinguish scenes that leave a question for later from endings that close the central question.

<a id="d02"></a>

## D02 Separate what each character wants from how they pursue it

Distinguish the desired result from the method used to obtain it from another person. Treat shifts between asking, testing, concealing, and other methods as possible breaks in dialogue or composition.

<a id="d03"></a>

## D03 Give dialogue a verb that acts on the listener

Consider what response a line seeks from the other person. When exposition continues uninterrupted, check the speaker's purpose and the effect on the listener's actions.

<a id="d04"></a>

## D04 Separate spoken words, concealed feelings, and inarticulate desires

Design separately what is said aloud, what is consciously concealed, and what the character cannot yet make sense of. Do not reveal everything through internal monologue; decide what the images can convey.

Preserve relationships and values the character will not abandon, as well as flaws. Do not reduce mixed emotions to a single word. Examine agreement, hesitation, and next action separately.

<a id="d05"></a>

## D05 Turn exposition into information characters use

Tie explanation to an occasion that requires information, such as a question, demand, or refusal. Show what becomes possible after hearing it. Repetition alone does not establish understanding.

Delay knowledge the story does not yet require until it is needed. Use [Setup and answering reader anticipation](03-beat-pacing-timing.md#reader-anticipation) to check whether readers also have a reason to want that explanation.

<a id="d06"></a>

## D06 Break long speeches at reactions

Split long dialogue where the listener's understanding or reaction changes. Do not mechanically divide unchanged content into shorter pieces that merely multiply balloons.

<a id="d07"></a>

## D07 Let a third thing carry the real dispute

Try expressing a hard-to-discuss issue through a shared task or the handling of possessions. If the prop alone does not convey the circumstances, add clues before or after it.

<a id="d08"></a>

## D08 Point words and actions in different directions

Separate the self-image a character protects with words from feelings revealed by the body or behavior. When speech and action conflict, decide which is a clue to their true feelings and support that meaning through reactions or repetition. Do not confuse meaningful inconsistency with drawing or continuity errors.

<a id="d09"></a>

## D09 Give short replies and hesitations a function

Even a short reply can accept, defer, refuse, or probe. Use posture and actions around the reply as well as ellipses.

<a id="d10"></a>

## D10 Give each character their own language and gestures

Consider explanation length, avoided topics, objects touched, and use of eye contact, not just catchphrases or sentence endings. Do not crowd out information by emphasizing the same habit every time.

Compare ordinary behavior with behavior after an event. Constantly maximal expressions make small changes harder to distinguish. Do not standardize intensity; identify exactly what changed.

**Voice examples illustrate a way of speaking; they are not required lines.** Treat examples in briefs or character sheets, such as “you know,” “I mean,” or “I think,” as clues to phrasing, politeness, and vocabulary. Do not append them to every line or copy the example verbatim. Write fresh dialogue suited to the scene's listener, purpose, and emotion. Omit a sample phrase when it does not fit. Quoted dialogue in a synopsis may also be rephrased for the flow unless the brief explicitly locks the wording, such as a line written on a form. The final dialogue record should distinguish wording retained exactly from newly written lines.

<a id="d11"></a>

## D11 Connect everyday actions to emotion

Use an ordinary task stopping, speeding up, or being restarted as an emotional clue. Choose depictions whose changes remain readable even without knowledge of the task.

If adding several everyday scenes, show a different relationship change in each. Repeating pleasant moments alone does not establish a deeper relationship. Give unoccupied views and daily actions a specific change in time, place, or state to convey.

<a id="d12"></a>

## D12 Give props multiple functions

A prop can establish a location, motivate an action, or show a relationship changing. If its return matters, give it a recognizable feature and a role on its first appearance.

<a id="d13"></a>

## D13 Let the location generate action

Design the place to affect distance between people, movement, and ease of conversation. With identical dialogue, compare whether an open or confined setting better serves the scene.

<a id="d14"></a>

## D14 Redirect scenes through entrances, sounds, and interruptions

When an arrival, departure, or noise interrupts, show whose choice changes as a result. If it merely decorates the conversation, also consider a version without it.

Even if the immediate business changes, track how the original problem remains. Do not equate an interrupted conversation with a resolved issue. At each break, check what understanding or payoff was delivered and whether the question left for later cancels it out.
