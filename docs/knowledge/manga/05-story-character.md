# 05 Story, Character, and Theme

Updated: 2026-09-17. Checks for comic production and review. These are production proposals to select according to the project. For related film and screenwriting material, see [Sources and scope](../manga-sources.md).

<a id="s01"></a>

## S01 State the protagonist, goal, resistance, and stakes in one sentence

Say who wants what, what prevents it, and what they lose if they fail. Check for a problem that makes readers want to follow the character, beyond the novelty of the premise.

<a id="s02"></a>

## S02 Show theme through choices and consequences

Design actions and consequences around the question the work explores. Before stating the conclusion in dialogue, check whether it can be read from the final choice.

<a id="s03"></a>

## S03 Define a desire and the belief that obstructs it

Separate what the character wants from the assumption limiting their behavior. If the story changes that assumption, place experiences that lead to the new judgment in intermediate scenes.

- Separate the immediate task from the desire the character prioritizes. Create choices that follow logically from the character's beliefs even when others find them strange.
- If a strength creates difficulties, change its scope or degree of application. Do not simply erase the ability to weaken the character.

<a id="s04"></a>

## S04 Turn character reference into differences in behavior

Translate character settings into different responses to trouble. Rather than expanding lists of ages and histories, create different choices in response to the same problem.

A character highly skilled at helping others may hesitate to ask for something they want. Do not force ability, knowledge, and interpersonal confidence to the same strength or weakness. Conversely, when a strength caused a failure, preserve the established strength and check what other goal the character was pursuing simultaneously.

Remembering another person's earlier small action and changing one's present response can be evidence of interest in that person. However, that one action does not establish the precise kind of feeling.

<a id="s05"></a>

## S05 Reveal character through choices under pressure

Include situations where desired outcomes conflict, not only easy choices. Depict what the character prioritized and what they lost, through to the consequences.

<a id="s06"></a>

## S06 Give the opposing side a coherent purpose

Give an opponent a desired outcome and a reason for it. Go beyond the role of inconveniencing the protagonist and clarify where the two goals cannot coexist.

<a id="s07"></a>

## S07 Do not skip the process of change

Before a major shift in attitude, include the necessary hesitation, small attempts, or failures. You need not show everything; preserve the material needed to understand the final choice.

- When a character changes their mind, connect the new fact, reassessment, and choice. Do not change behavior on an emotional declaration alone.
- Noticing another person's condition and stopping or changing a planned action can also demonstrate recognition.

<a id="s08"></a>

## S08 Let earlier actions create the next difficulty

Check that earlier decisions create later situations instead of merely placing incidents in sequence. Where coincidences accumulate, consider replacing them with consequences of the characters' own actions.

<a id="s09"></a>

## S09 Open with a situation in motion and a reason to watch

Give readers a character to follow, the current situation, and a question about what comes next. Supply only the world information needed for understanding instead of explaining everything first.

<a id="s10"></a>

## S10 Give setup a natural role on its first appearance

Information important later should also have a natural purpose when first introduced. Compare thumbnail versions where it is too easy to miss and where it reveals the conclusion too clearly.

Preserve established facts while later information updates the interpretation of purpose, cause, value, or relationships. Do not invent a rule only at the end to justify earlier results.

If showing the outcome first, connect the question readers follow with the explanation supplied later. Do not call incomprehensibility from excessive concealment a surprise.

Distinguish explaining a fact, completing an action, reversing meaning, updating a relationship, and making a theme concrete. A returning prop or line alone does not constitute payoff. Provide a cue to remember the earlier information and show its newly understood meaning.

Tell readers the relevant properties of a solution when they need them to understand the result. Do not assume readers received a premise merely because the author or analyst knows it.

<a id="s11"></a>

## S11 Concentrate the climax in an action or image

At the resolution, choose an action that represents the character's choice and the relationship change. Before adding explanation, check what the image and reaction alone convey.

### Examine three states separately

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| External situation | What stopped, what was achieved, and what remains unresolved? |
| The character's understanding | What did they learn, reconsider, and choose next? |
| Relationship with the other person | Whose response, agreement, or voluntary participation established what? |

Do not substitute one resolved event for everyone's acceptance. Nor must every dimension be resolved. Distinguish what is settled, what still needs resolution, and the future deliberately left open.

### Preserve the basis for choice and acceptance

When conditions for continuing a conflict change, connect each person's choice to continue, stop, or cooperate to its basis. Give reasons for bystanders to intervene or refrain as well.

Understanding another person's motive differs from excusing their behavior. Do not explain away harm or responsibility. Do not establish a relationship solely through one person's wishes; leave the other person opportunities to refuse, set conditions, and respond. Everyone need not agree simultaneously.

Check whether behavior after the outcome shows a response previously impossible, or changed distance and responsibilities in the relationship. Try removing the stated lesson to see whether character change remains readable.

### When using humor, everyday life, or supplementary material

Humor should not undo the central desire, emotion, or outcome. One option is to follow it with an outsider's reaction or a small practical matter. Genre supervision addresses specific comedy techniques.

If returning to everyday life, check changes in attitude, responsibilities, and distance. Do not erase loss to convey hope, and check that sorrow alone does not close off the future. Do not standardize the length of the aftermath or the ending's composition.

Before adding supplementary material, check that the main story establishes the essential understanding on its own. Identify the emotion, relationship, or choice the supplement adds. This is not an instruction to add an epilogue automatically.

### Check what has been established and which conditions remain

Examine achievements, lasting effects on ability or the body, treatment by others, and the character's next goal separately. Difficulties may remain after success, and information or relationships may change after failure. Even when later reinterpreting an achievement's value, preserve what actually happened.

Compare what the character can and cannot do before and after the result. The larger the outcome, the more important it is to check that the next scene does not return to exactly the same behavior as before.

<a id="s12"></a>

## S12 Match structure to the intended reading experience

Use structural methods to find problems, not to allocate pages mechanically by fixed percentages. Distribute space for the intended experience of a short story, serial, slice-of-life work, or other form.
