# Story Structure and Scene Design

Sources checked: 2026-09-12. Applies to organizing plots, episode structure, character roles, and scene purposes and outcomes.

## Using this document

Distinguish theories confirmed in the sources from production suggestions for adapting them to comics. Do not use them as scoring rules that force every story into one structure. In slice-of-life stories, open-ended stories, or stories about failure and loss, an unmet goal or a changed outlook can also be an outcome.

## Use patterns of change to develop scenes

The publicly available excerpt of Propp's English translation includes interdiction and violation, and the villain's trickery and the victim's complicity. See the [原典の公開抜粋](https://www.perlego.com/book/3273841/morphology-of-the-folk-tale-pdf). Takashi Ogata's paper discusses function pairs and their reconstruction: [小方孝の論文](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jcss/14/4/14_4_532/_pdf/-char/ja). Read the original theory separately from a researcher's interpretation.

The following are possible production approaches to building scenes. They do not claim that any particular theory defines only these five categories.

| Possible change | What to design | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Obtain something missing or recover something lost | Desire, means, and the change after obtaining it | Are acquisition and recovery being treated as the same outcome? |
| Choose a forbidden action | Reason for the prohibition, temptation, and cost of the choice | Has the character's goal disappeared behind the violation itself? |
| Devise and try a plan | Expectations, ingenuity, and the other party's response | Can readers understand why it succeeds or fails? |
| Tackle a difficult task | Obstacles, attempts, cooperation, and the endpoint | Are the effects of effort, luck, and help being confused? |
| Avoid danger or recover from harm | Warning of danger, choices, and consequences | Is the information needed for prevention distinguished from that needed for recovery? |

## Organize characters by role

Louis Hébert's explanation organizes Greimas's actants as subject/object, helper/opponent, and sender/receiver, including overlapping roles and nonhuman elements: [The Actantial Model](https://www.signosemio.com/pages/greimas/actantial-model.php). The table below translates those roles into questions for comic design.

| Role | Production question |
| --- | --- |
| Acting subject | Whose choices will readers follow? |
| Desired object | What do they want to obtain, protect, or change? |
| Impetus for action | Why must they act now? |
| Recipient of the outcome | Who is affected by success or failure? |
| Helper | Is support provided by a person, skill, tool, or chance? |
| Opponent | Is the obstacle another person, the environment, an institution, or the character's own assumption? |

One character may serve several roles. Before adding a character, check how that character affects the scene's choices and outcomes.

## Develop an episode into panels

1. Choose the emotion readers should retain and the character they will follow.
2. For each scene, write "initial state → action → obstacle → choice → outcome."
3. Check whether the preceding choice creates the next situation.
4. Separate what characters know from the order in which readers learn it.
5. Specify visible actions and text in the panel script, then check reading order and density in thumbnails.
6. Read the story through and repair missing motivations or reactions, and passages that repeat the same function.

When checking with readers, ask what the character wanted, why they made their choice, and what changed. Treat understanding separately from preference. Keep normal production decisions in review records; organize them as feedback only when a feedback record is requested.
